04

What was known when the decision was made?

A trading method receives information, chooses a portfolio, and later sees the result. The order matters. A decision time is the point at which the method must act. Its information cutoff is the latest time from which it may use data.

A five-minute difference

Suppose a decision is made at 09:00. News published at 08:50 is already available. News published at 09:05 is not. Both items have the same calendar date, but they belong on different sides of the decision.

RecordWhen it becomes availableKnown at 09:00?
Yesterday's closing priceAfter yesterday's market closeYes
News itemToday, 09:05No
Today's closing priceAfter today's market closeNo

These times explain the idea; they do not define the course trading schedule.

Financial reports also have two times: the period they describe and the date they are released. A report about last year's earnings may not be public until several months later. A time zone can also change which event came first.

Using later information in an earlier decision is called lookahead bias. An LLM creates another possible route: it may remember what happened to a named company. Removing names and dates can reduce this clue, but a distinctive event or number may still reveal the history.

05

What do the input numbers mean?

StockBench brings together prices, company information, and news. These describe different parts of the market. The course starter includes the released local data; individual fields may be missing. A missing value means the information is unavailable; it does not mean the value is zero.

InputPlain meaning
Open and closePrices at the start and end of a trading session.
Trading volumeThe number of shares traded during a period.
Recent returnsPrice changes measured relative to earlier prices.
Earnings per shareCompany profit, under a stated accounting definition, divided by the relevant share count.
NewsA report about an event, with a publication time and source.

A price is a dollar amount for one share. A return is a relative change. For Stock A, a move from $50 to $52 gives a price return of ($52 − $50) / $50 = 4%. This does not yet include its $1 dividend.

One common company measure is the price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E:

P/E = share price / earnings per share

A number needs context

At a $50 price and $2 annual earnings per share, Stock A has P/E = 50 / 2 = 25. A lower P/E does not prove that a stock is a better buy. Expected growth, business risk, and the earnings period may differ. The usual positive-earnings interpretation also breaks down when earnings are zero or negative.

Past earnings and forecast earnings are different inputs. News describes events and expectations; its tone is not a direct measurement of the next price move.

06

From a decision to a portfolio

A portfolio contains cash and shares. Its value changes when prices move, dividends arrive, or costs are paid. For our two fictional stocks:

V = C + qAPA + qBPB

V is total value, C is cash, q is the number of shares, and P is the share price. A stock's weight is its holding value divided by V.

One account, three parts

With $200 cash, 10 A shares at $50, and 15 B shares at $20, the account is worth $1,000. A has weight 50%, B has weight 30%, and cash has weight 20%.

If no shares are traded, A later reaches $52 and pays $1 per share, while B falls to $19. Cash becomes $210. The account is then worth $210 + 10 × $52 + 15 × $19 = $1,015, before trading costs.

A target is the desired holding after a trade, rather than the amount to buy. In the course starter, a strategy returns share quantities: positive to buy and negative to sell. The orders_from_weights helper can turn target portfolio weights into orders. The original LLM adapter also uses target_cash_amount, which means the target dollar value held in a stock; it does not mean uninvested cash.

Trading costs and basis points

A basis point, or bp, is 0.01%. Thus 10 bps = 0.10% = 0.001.

Consider a separate trade at the starting prices: sell 2 A shares for $100 and buy 5 B shares for $100. Total traded dollars are $200. At an illustrative fee of 10 bps on each trade, the fee is $200 × 0.001 = $0.20. Cash falls to $199.80, and the account is worth $999.80 immediately after trading.

One common definition of turnover is total traded dollars divided by portfolio value before the trades. Here it is 200 / 1,000 = 20%. Some reports use half this amount. Fee rates, execution prices, and trade constraints depend on the runner's rules.

07

Reading the result

Cumulative return is the total change from the starting account value. With no money added or withdrawn:

R = Vend / Vstart − 1

Here R is cumulative return. The account ending at $1,015 has R = 1,015 / 1,000 − 1 = 1.5%. Returns over several days compound; simply adding them generally gives a different answer.

Drawdown measures a fall from a previous peak. Maximum drawdown is the largest such fall in the observed path:

drawdown = 1 − current value / highest value so far

A path of $1,000 → $1,100 → $990 → $1,015 ends with a 1.5% gain but has a 10% maximum drawdown, because 1 − 990 / 1,100 = 10%. Some software, including StockBench's summary, reports this fall as −10%.

Downside risk focuses on returns below a chosen target. The Sortino ratio compares average return above that target with a measure of how much returns fall below the target. A target could be 0% per day. Its value depends on the target, time unit, and exact formula. StockBench uses its own calculation, so a number from another package may not match.

A benchmark is a clearly defined reference portfolio. Its market, dates, cash treatment, and costs affect the comparison. It gives context to a return; it is not a promise of what was easy to earn.

Prediction accuracy counts correct answers. Profit also depends on price-move size and invested dollars. Many correct predictions of small gains can be outweighed by a few large losses.
