3.1 / Portfolio risk

How a mix changes risk

A portfolio's future return is unknown. An expected return is an average of possible future returns, weighted by their probabilities. In practice, we estimate it from data or a model. It is not a promised return.

If all the money is invested in two stocks, the expected return is:

μp = wAμA + wBμB

Here, μ means expected return, p means portfolio, and w is the share of money in each stock. The weights add to one. All returns must refer to the same period.

Variance measures the average squared distance from the expected return. Its square root, the standard deviation σ, has the same units as returns. Correlation ρ describes how two returns move together. It ranges from −1 to +1. It changes the risk of their mix:

σp2 = wA2σA2 + wB2σB2 + 2wAwBρσAσB

Two stocks, equal weights

This is a separate, imagined one-month model for Stock A and Stock B. Each gets half the money. Estimated expected returns are 1% and 0.5%; both standard deviations are 10%.

Expected portfolio return = 0.5 × 1% + 0.5 × 0.5% = 0.75%.

Estimated correlationPortfolio standard deviation
ρ = 110%
ρ = 0√(0.5² × 0.1² + 0.5² × 0.1²) ≈ 7.07%

The estimated mean stays the same, but the spread changes. These numbers describe this model, not known future outcomes.

Holding more names helps less when they move together. Correlation can change, and zero correlation does not imply independence. A lower standard deviation also does not remove every type of loss.

3.2 / Market returns and beta

What moved with the market?

A stock can rise because many stocks rose that day. It can also move because of company news. These effects are hard to separate perfectly, but a simple model gives us a starting language:

ri,t = αi + βirm,t + εi,t

Here, ri,t is stock i's return in period t. The market index return is rm,t. Beta β measures how strongly the stock moves with that index in the fitted model. Here, “fitted” means estimated from past data. Alpha α is the constant term. The remaining part ε is the residual: what this model does not explain.

A market move and a remaining part

For a separate period, suppose Stock A has fitted β = 1.5 and α = 0. If the market rises 2%, the model's market-linked part is 1.5 × 2% = 3%. If A rises 3.5%, the residual for that period is 0.5 percentage points.

If the market falls, that same positive beta can work in the other direction. Beta describes a fitted relationship; it does not promise tomorrow's response.

A risk factor is a common source of return changes. Models can include several factors. For example, researchers use market returns and returns of stock groups based on company size or value measures.

In this setting, exposure means how sensitive a portfolio is to a factor. High returns may partly reflect such exposure. A positive fitted alpha is not proof of skill: the sample may be short, costs may be missing, or the chosen model may leave out other drivers.

3.3 / Information and learning

Information, expectations, and learning

A price reflects trades made by people with different information and beliefs. Public news may already be widely known when a model reads it. The idea of market efficiency asks how much available information is already reflected in prices. It does not mean that prices are always correct.

Good news can disappoint

People expect Stock A's company profit to grow by 10%. It reports 5% growth. Profit grew, but the result is below the earlier expectation by 5 percentage points. The stock price may fall. Reading “profit increased” alone misses this difference.

Markets also change as policies, participants, and tools change. This connects finance to several learning ideas:

  • Ensembles combine views. Their errors may still overlap if they use the same information.
  • Online learning updates a method after feedback arrives. Fast changes can respond to new conditions but also to noise.
  • LLMs can turn text into useful information. Understanding a sentence does not mean its price effect is known.
  • Agents organize tools, memory, and decisions. More steps do not automatically produce better decisions.
